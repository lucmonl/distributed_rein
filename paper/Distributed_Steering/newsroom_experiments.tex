\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Task, attribute and clients}
\label{sec:setup-data}

\paragraph{Task.}
We study single-document news summarization on Newsroom \citep{grusky-etal-2018-newsroom}, and steer the \emph{extractiveness} of the generated summary.
Extractiveness is measured by fragment density \citep{grusky-etal-2018-newsroom}.
Let $\mathcal{F}(A,S)$ be the set of maximal shared token sequences (extractive fragments) between article $A$ and summary $S$.
Then
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{density}(A,S) = \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(A,S)} |f|^{2}.
\end{equation}
Density is low when a summary rewrites its source, and high when it copies long spans.
We reimplemented the scorer with a regex tokenizer. Its Spearman correlation with the density values distributed with Newsroom is 0.99.
Articles are truncated to 400 words, and density is recomputed on the truncated article.
Pairs whose summary depends on the removed text are dropped.
The same scorer labels the training data and evaluates generated summaries.

\paragraph{Clients.}
Each publication is a client.
We take the 12 publications with at least 5{,}000 usable pairs whose median densities are evenly spaced, from telegraph.co.uk (median 1.3) to nypost.com (32.0).
Table~\ref{tab:clients} lists them.
The publications are split into three held-out rotations of four clients, stratified by density.
All results here use rotation~0: eight \emph{participants} train the shared control mechanism, and four \emph{held-out} clients join afterwards.
The publications simulate organizations; this is not a deployment involving the publishers.

\paragraph{Splits.}
Each client has a training pool of up to 5{,}000 pairs, a development split of 100 articles, and a test split of 200 articles taken from Newsroom's official test split (180 for theguardian.com).
Participants train on 4{,}000 pairs each unless stated otherwise.
Training, development and test data come from years before each client's latest year, which is held back.
That later data is not used in the experiments reported here.

\paragraph{Data characteristics.}
Two properties of the raw data matter for interpretation.
First, 92\% of nypost.com's raw summaries are its first ${\sim}200$ characters followed by an ellipsis, a fixed format taken from page metadata.
We keep them, and treat nypost.com as a client with a hard format convention.
Second, density is partly coupled to length (Spearman 0.64 on the training data), so we report output length as an off-target measure.

\subsection{Shared control scale}
\label{sec:setup-alpha}

A requested level $\alpha\in[0,1]$ is a percentile on a scale shared by all clients.
Let $a(y)$ be the density of summary $y$ and $F_i$ the empirical distribution of $a$ on participant $i$'s training data.
The reference distribution is the equal-weight mixture $F=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F_i$ over the $M=8$ participants.
The server can build $F$ by averaging normalized per-client histograms, so no examples leave a client.
Every training pair is labelled $\alpha(y)=F(a(y))$, and held-out clients reuse the same $F$.
Under this scale, $\alpha=0.5$ requests the same behaviour on every client.
A client's data covers only part of $[0,1]$.
Its \emph{support} is the interval between the 5th and 95th percentiles of its own labels.
Requests outside the support are \emph{out-of-support}.
Values there are scarce in the client's data, although not necessarily absent.

\subsection{Model and federated training}
\label{sec:setup-model}

\paragraph{Parameterization.}
Every attention and MLP projection with frozen weight $W_0$ of client $i$ computes
\begin{equation}
  y = W_0x + B_i^{P}A_i^{P}x + g(\alpha)\,B^{D}A^{D}x .
  \label{eq:steerlinear}
\end{equation}
$B_i^{P}A_i^{P}$ is a private rank-16 LoRA adapter that never leaves the client.
$B^{D}A^{D}$ is the shared rank-16 steering direction $D$.
$A^{D}$ is a random matrix generated from a common seed and kept frozen; only $B^{D}$ is trained, starting at zero.
Since $A^{D}$ is identical across clients, averaging $B^{D}$ averages the direction products exactly.
The calibration $g$ maps the requested level to an intervention strength:
\begin{align}
  g(\alpha) &= s\,h(\alpha), \\
  h(\alpha) &= (1-w)\,\alpha + w\bigl[1-(1-\alpha^{p})^{q}\bigr].
\end{align}
Here $s=\exp(u)\in[\tfrac14,4]$ is a gain, and $h$ is a monotone map with $h(0)=0$ and $h(1)=1$, initialized at the identity.
The calibration is \emph{shared}: one $(u,w,p,q)$ for all clients, aggregated with $B^{D}$.
There is no offset, so $g(0)=0$.

\paragraph{Objective.}
Each client minimizes the negative log-likelihood of its reference summaries with steering active at the summary's own level,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_i = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim S_i}\bigl[-\log p_{W_i(\alpha(y))}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
over $B_i^{P}, A_i^{P}$, its copy of $B^{D}$ and the calibration.
Task adaptation and control are therefore learned in a single loss, and the direction is trained in the presence of the private adapters it must act on.

\paragraph{Federated protocol.}
We use FedAvg with full participation of the eight participants.
In each round, every client takes 20 local AdamW steps with batch size 8.
The server then averages $B^{D}$ and the calibration parameters uniformly across clients, so large clients do not dominate.
Private adapters and their optimizer states persist across rounds; the optimizer state of shared parameters is reset each round.
Training runs for 100 rounds.
Learning rates are $2\times10^{-4}$ for the adapter and direction and $10^{-2}$ for the gain and warp, with 20 warm-up steps.
The gain is trained from round 2 and the warp from round 5, with a penalty $0.01\cdot\overline{(h(a)-a)^2}$ toward the identity.
Prompts are truncated to 1{,}024 tokens and targets to 128 tokens.

\paragraph{Backbones.}
The main backbone is Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct.
We replicate the participant comparison on Qwen3-8B, with thinking disabled.
Both use identical data and hyperparameters.

\subsection{Compared methods}
\label{sec:setup-baselines}

All methods share the backbone, client data, $\alpha$ labels and adapter placement.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\fed{} (ours).} The method above.
  \item \textbf{\loc{}.} The same model, loss and step budget, but nothing is aggregated.
  Each client learns its own direction and its own gain and warp from its own data.
  \item \textbf{B1: prompting.} On each client's federated model with the direction removed ($B^{D}=0$), the requested level is stated in the prompt as a 0--100 extractiveness target.
  The prompt has either no examples ($k=0$) or $k=3$ examples from the client's own training data nearest to the target level.
  We also report $k=3$ on the base model without an adapter.
  \item \textbf{B3: one-shot merge.} The uniform average of the \loc{} clients' directions, attached to each client's local adapter and calibration.
  \item \textbf{B4: federated activation steering.} Contrastive activation addition \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering}.
  Each client computes a mean-difference vector at the middle layer between its top- and bottom-quartile summaries, and the server averages these vectors.
  Each client then fits one gain on 30 development articles, and steering adds $s(2\alpha-1)\,\bar{\lVert h\rVert}\,\hat v$ to the residual stream.
  Like B1, it runs on the client's model with the direction removed.
\end{itemize}
B1 and B4 therefore keep the client's private adapter and change only the control mechanism.

\paragraph{Held-out clients.}
A new client joins with its first $n$ training pairs, for $n\in\{16, 64, 256, 1024, \text{all}\}$; the subsets are nested.
Its $\alpha$ labels come from the participants' reference $F$ at no cost.
We compare four ways for it to obtain control:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Frozen $D$.} The participants' direction and shared calibration are frozen. The client trains a new private adapter with the participants' objective, i.e.\ with steering active at each pair's level.
  \item \textbf{Local $D$.} The same objective and number of steps, but the client also learns its own direction and calibration from zero.
  \item \textbf{Plug-in.} The adapter is trained by plain fine-tuning with steering off, and the frozen direction is attached afterwards.
  \item \textbf{Prompting.} The plug-in adapter without the direction, with the level in the prompt and three examples drawn from the $n$ pairs.
\end{itemize}
All settings take $\mathrm{clip}(\lceil 4n/8\rceil, 200, 2000)$ steps: 200 for $n\le256$, 512 for $n=1024$, and 2{,}000 for the full pool.
Four epochs match the participants' training budget.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and metrics}
\label{sec:setup-eval}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
During training, every 10th round's checkpoint is evaluated on the development split (100 articles $\times$ 5 levels).
We select the checkpoint with the lowest mean percentile error and report on test.
At test time each client generates a summary for each of its test articles at $\alpha\in\{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$ with greedy decoding (at most 128 new tokens).
Client-level metrics are averaged with equal client weights.
We also report the worst client.

\paragraph{Control.}
The primary metric is the \emph{percentile error} $\lvert F(a(\hat y_\alpha))-\alpha\rvert$, averaged over articles and levels.
It is bounded and scale-free.
A model that always outputs the median scores $0.30$ on this grid, so a useful control must stay below that.
We split the error into in-support and out-of-support requests.
\emph{Reach} is the share of out-of-support outputs that leave the client's own support in the requested direction.
Ordering is measured by the per-article Spearman correlation between $\alpha$ and output density.
Articles whose output does not change with $\alpha$ count as 0.
The \emph{near-tie} rate is the share of adjacent levels whose outputs are identical after normalizing quotes, whitespace and a trailing ellipsis, allowing a token-level edit ratio $\geq 0.95$.
It guards against unchanged outputs that appear steerable, such as truncated leads cut at different positions.

\paragraph{Quality.}
Extractive summaries are easier to score as faithful, so quality is always compared with \emph{real summaries at the same requested level} (binned by $\alpha$).
This follows the matched-extractiveness protocol of \citet{ladhak-etal-2022-faithful}.
We report factual consistency with AlignScore-large \citep{zha-etal-2023-alignscore}, BERTScore F1 against the reference \citep{zhang-etal-2020-bertscore}, and length.
On 25 articles per client and level, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct also judges faithfulness at the sentence level, following FineSurE \citep{song-etal-2024-finesure}, and rates relevance and coherence on a 1--5 scale; it is blind to method and level.

\paragraph{House style.}
To test whether a client's outputs keep its publication's style independently of extractiveness, we train a publication classifier on the participants' real training summaries.
It is logistic regression on character 2--5-gram and word 1--2-gram TF-IDF features.
The training set is \emph{density-matched}: within each of five $\alpha$ bins, every publication contributes the same number of summaries (at most 400), so extractiveness cannot identify the publication.
\emph{Attribution} is the share of a client's in-support outputs that the classifier assigns to its own publication; chance is 0.125.
We also report a \emph{style-feature gap}, which is lower when outputs are closer to the client's style.
It is the absolute standardized mean difference between generated summaries and the client's real summaries at a similar level ($\pm0.125$ in $\alpha$), averaged over 11 interpretable features.
The features are sentence count, words per sentence, word length, quotes, ellipses, parentheses, numerals, capitalized words, first- and second-person pronouns, and question and exclamation marks.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.}
Method comparisons use the same test articles and levels.
Per client, we compute a 95\% paired bootstrap interval over articles (4{,}000 resamples) for the difference in percentile error, and count clients whose interval excludes zero.

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares federated training with local training at equal data and steps.
On Llama-3.2-1B, \fed{} lowers the mean percentile error from 0.165 to 0.151 and the worst-client out-of-support error from 0.249 to 0.188.
It also increases the per-article Spearman correlation from 0.906 to 0.933 and reduces near-ties from 0.246 to 0.204.
\fed{} is significantly better than \loc{} on four of eight clients and significantly worse on none.
On in-support requests alone, it is better on six.
The result replicates on a second model family and scale.
On Qwen3-8B, \fed{} reduces the mean error from 0.177 to 0.156 and is significantly better on six of eight clients.
The 2{,}000-pair setting shows the same direction: 0.162 for \fed{} versus 0.180 for \loc{}, significantly better on six clients.
That pair used per-client calibration with an offset.

\paragraph{The gains are largest where a client's own data does not cover the request.}
Table~\ref{tab:perclient} breaks the comparison down by client.
The largest improvements occur for the two clients missing large parts of the scale, mainly on out-of-support requests.
theguardian.com has almost no extractive summaries (support $[0.04, 0.55]$).
Its out-of-support error falls by 0.101 on 1B (95\% CI $[-0.118, -0.084]$) and by 0.137 on 8B.
nypost.com has almost no abstractive summaries (support $[0.51, 0.95]$).
On 1B, its out-of-support error falls by 0.040 ($[-0.051, -0.030]$).
In the 2{,}000-pair pair, theguardian.com's mean output percentile at $\alpha=0,\dots,1$ is $0.18, 0.31, 0.49, 0.75, 0.90$ under \fed{} and $0.11, 0.37, 0.48, 0.57, 0.63$ under \loc{}.
Its local model stalls near the top of its own data, while the federated model reaches the 90th percentile of the shared scale.
For clients whose data already spans nearly the whole scale, ``out-of-support'' refers only to the 5\% tails, and \fed{} is slightly worse there (up to $+0.03$ on 1B).
Collaboration therefore helps most where a client's data has gaps in coverage, consistent with the premise that clients with complementary coverage benefit from a shared direction.

\paragraph{Iterative federated training is needed, and the learned knob beats simpler control.}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares the method with alternative control mechanisms.
Averaging independently learned local directions once (B3) gives an error of 0.184.
That is worse than both \fed{} and \loc{}, and \fed{} is better on seven of eight clients.
A merged direction does not match the adapters and calibrations it is attached to.
Federated activation steering (B4) orders outputs only partially (Spearman 0.752).
It lowers AlignScore out of support by 0.21 relative to real summaries at the same level, and lengthens summaries by 11.5 tokens.
Stating the target level in the prompt (B1) does not steer density with this backbone: Spearman is near zero.
Without examples, 70\% of articles give near-identical summaries at $\alpha=0$ and $\alpha=1$.
Fragment density is a corpus statistic that a model cannot easily estimate for its own output, so a learned control mechanism is needed.

\paragraph{Control comes without a quality cost.}
Table~\ref{tab:quality} shows that federated and local models have comparable consistency, BERTScore and judged faithfulness.
Both stay close to real summaries requested at the same level.
Absolute AlignScore varies with extractiveness even for real summaries: from 0.48 in the most abstractive bin to 0.84--0.87 in extractive bins.
This is why we compare against the same-level reference.
Out of support, the federated models are judged more relevant than the local ones (relevance gap $+0.02$ vs.\ $-0.05$ on 1B), and their outputs are 2--3 tokens longer than the reference.
This reflects the coupling of density and length as they reach higher levels.

Collaboration should not erase what distinguishes a client's writing.
Table~\ref{tab:style} measures publication style with a classifier trained so that extractiveness carries no information about the publication.
The method's outputs are attributed to their own publication 55.2\% of the time, close to real test summaries (58.7\%) and to locally trained models (55.3\%).
Sharing the direction therefore does not wash out private style.
Making the adapter shared as well, so that nothing is personalized, lowers attribution to 40.9\% and nearly doubles the feature gap (0.190 vs.\ 0.108).
Attribution drops for all eight clients, most for nypost.com (0.67 to 0.25) and theguardian.com (0.55 to 0.29).
Two observations locate the style in the private adapter.
Prompting on the client model, which keeps the adapter, retains attribution (0.556).
The same prompts and in-context examples on the base model do not (0.274).
Style is also orthogonal to the steered attribute: the method's attribution stays between 0.52 and 0.56 across requested levels from $\alpha=0$ to $1$.
The advantage over the shared adapter is present from round 10 onward, so it does not come from late-round memorization.
The 8B models show the same pattern (0.559 federated, 0.577 local).
A fully shared adapter extrapolates better out of support (0.136 vs.\ 0.161) at the cost of in-support control (0.177 vs.\ 0.135) and house style.
Private adaptation is thus the choice that preserves client characteristics while retaining the collaboration gains above.

A direction that works only on the models it was co-trained with would be of limited use.
Table~\ref{tab:e2} tests reuse on four clients that took no part in federation.
With the participants' direction and calibration frozen, a new client that trains only its private adapter on 16 pairs reaches an error of 0.179.
Learning its own direction from the same 16 pairs and steps gives 0.295, close to the constant-output level of 0.30.
Frozen $D$ is significantly better on all four held-out clients at every budget from 16 to 1{,}024 pairs (Table~\ref{tab:e2-perclient}).
At $n=16$, the per-client differences range from $-0.094$ to $-0.156$.
With 16 pairs, the frozen direction already has a lower mean error than a locally learned direction with 1{,}024 pairs (0.192).
At $n\le256$, both settings reach near-zero training loss in the same 200 steps, so local $D$'s deficit reflects sample efficiency rather than under-training.
The gap narrows as local data grows.
At full data, frozen $D$ is still better on two clients and ties on the other two, and its mean error (0.135) is below the participants' own (0.151).

\paragraph{Joining requires training with the direction active.}
The frozen direction transfers through an adapter trained with it.
Attaching the direction to an adapter fine-tuned without it (plug-in) fails at every budget (0.34--0.38).
Its outputs at $\alpha=0$ already sit high on the scale and are 26--37 tokens longer than real summaries at the same level.
Joining therefore needs no new data type: the new client labels its pairs with the public reference $F$ and trains its adapter with steering active, exactly as the participants did.
Prompting stays above the constant-output reference.

\paragraph{Quality.}
Frozen and local $D$ have equal summary quality.
AlignScore gaps to the same-level reference are within 0.04 of each other at every $n$, BERTScore is 0.885--0.900 for both, and judged out-of-support relevance favours the frozen direction ($-0.03$ to $+0.03$ vs.\ $-0.04$ to $-0.15$).
The exception is length: with 16 pairs, frozen $D$ writes summaries 14 tokens longer than the reference out of support, which falls to 2--7 tokens from $n=256$.

The global scale gives $\alpha$ the same meaning on every client, so the mapping from requested level to intervention strength can also be shared.
Table~\ref{tab:calibration} shows that sharing it gives the best mean error, worst client, out-of-support error and Spearman correlation.
A per-client calibration can only be fitted where the client has data.
For broadly covered clients, it brings small in-support gains (at most $-0.015$).
It fails for the skewed client it is meant to help: nypost.com's error rises by 0.143 and its out-of-support error from 0.188 to 0.365.
Fitted only on $\alpha\in[0.51, 0.95]$, nypost.com's private calibration learns a small gain and a strongly bent warp.
Its outputs at $\alpha=0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1$ sit at percentiles $0.64, 0.70, 0.76, 0.85, 0.90$.
With the shared calibration they span $0.18, 0.41, 0.71, 0.84, 0.90$.
The shared calibration also carries over to new clients.
In the held-out setting, freezing it gives the same data-efficiency curve as training a private calibration on the new client's pairs:
$0.179, 0.170, 0.146, 0.139, 0.135$ versus $0.183, 0.177, 0.151, 0.141, 0.132$ for $n=16,\dots,\text{all}$.
A joining client therefore has no calibration parameters to fit.

\paragraph{Scope of the evidence.}
All results use one training seed and one held-out rotation, so the bootstrap intervals cover article sampling but not training or partition variance.
The four held-out clients of rotation~0 have broad support.
The held-out experiment therefore tests data efficiency more than transfer into coverage gaps.
In the local baselines, the calibration gain ends at its upper bound of 4 for six or seven of eight clients.
The direction's norm can compensate for this, but more slowly.
The federated shared gain stays well inside the bound (1.93 on 1B, 2.31 on 8B).
We keep the same bound for all runs.
